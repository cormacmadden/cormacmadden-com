## Context

This project was an experiment to see if I could make a car configurator in one weekend, using AI to speed up the non-creative parts of the workflow while preserving the true details of the car model.

## Where AI took the time out

- **Retargeting animations.** Moving animations onto a new rig is fiddly, rule-based work, the kind that is easy to describe and slow to do by hand.
- **Variant preparation.** Every paint and environment option has to be set up and kept consistent across the scene. AI handled the bulk of that preparation so the variants could be switched on one car.
- **Prototyping.** Templates, layouts and background examples were generated quickly, so I could try ideas and throw away the weak ones in minutes rather than hours.

## Materials and lighting

The car model is bought in; the materials are my own. The paint is a clear-coat material with metallic flake, set up once and recoloured for each variant, and the carbon fibre and suede are built to hold up in close-ups of the interior and engine bay. The scene is a plain concrete terrace under an open sky, so the reflections stay clean and the shape of the car does the work.

The configurator runs in real time. The stills are rendered with Unreal's path tracer, exported as raw 32-bit HDR images, then graded in Photoshop and exported for the web.

## The result

A working configurator, shown in the walkthrough below, and the stills on this page, all rendered in Unreal Engine 5: exterior and overhead shots, and the details that make an F1 an F1, down to the central driving seat.

The experiment answered its question: the repetitive setup behind a configurator is now the part AI does well, which leaves the time for materials, lighting and the final image.
